## Summary

SHARANG presents a potentially useful heterogeneous ARM–CUDA implementation strategy: NEON for latency-sensitive ML-KEM operations and GPU batching for throughput. However, its central FIPS-conformance and security-hardening claims are not currently substantiated. Several claims appear mathematically false or internally inconsistent, notably the NTT bound proofs, checksum invariant, shuffle entropy, classification of GPU KeyGen as a public-key operation, and the CUDA “KeyGen” output size.

The performance contribution might become publishable after extensive correction, reproducible release, and remeasurement of complete FIPS 203 operations. In its present form, the security narrative substantially overstates the evidence.

## Genuine Strengths

- Practical separation of CPU latency and GPU batch-throughput use cases.
- Platform, compiler, warmup, and iteration details are reported.
- Primitive-level timing and bottleneck breakdowns are potentially useful.
- Exact NEON Montgomery/Barrett arithmetic is preferable to unexplained approximate SIMD arithmetic.
- The manuscript acknowledges lack of DPA protection, non-constant-time GPU execution, and checksum-preserving multi-faults.
- KAT-style testing and CPU/GPU differential testing are appropriate starting points.

# Critical Findings

### C1. CUDA “KeyGen” is not demonstrated to be ML-KEM KeyGen

**Anchor:** Table I, lines 80–82; Algorithm 2, lines 284–305; headline results, lines 31–35.

Algorithm 2 accepts one 32-byte `coins` value and outputs `sk[N][1152]`. It appears to perform only K-PKE key generation. FIPS 203 ML-KEM-768 has a 2400-byte decapsulation key and requires independent `d` and `z`, followed by construction of:

`dkPKE || ek || H(ek) || z`.

Thus, the reported 6.6 ms and 3.1× result may compare GPU K-PKE key generation against complete CPU ML-KEM KeyGen.

**Minimum remedy:** Publish the exact call graph and benchmark boundaries. Implement and time complete FIPS 203 `ML-KEM.KeyGen_internal`, including `d`, `z`, hashes, full 2400-byte key construction, synchronization, and memory movement. Otherwise relabel the GPU operation and remove full-KEM comparisons.

---

### C2. Shuffle entropy and SPA-resistance claims are untenable

**Anchor:** Section V-A, lines 234–241; Section VIII-D, lines 433–435; Section IX-C, lines 589–597; conclusion, lines 608–615.

The schedule is deterministically seeded from only three polynomial coefficients and generated by xorshift32. Its realized entropy cannot exceed 32 bits and, conditioned on the polynomial, has zero fresh entropy. Counting all mathematical layer permutations does not establish entropy realized by a 32-bit generator.

For public or chosen inputs, schedules may be predictable; repeated inputs repeat schedules. For secret inputs, secret-derived memory indices can themselves introduce timing, cache, EM, or power leakage and contradict the constant-time claim. No leakage experiment demonstrates SPA resistance.

**Minimum remedy:** Withdraw “>1000 bits entropy,” “unpredictability,” “zero-cost SPA resistance,” and equivalent checkmarks. Use fresh independent cryptographic randomness with unbiased index generation, or describe the mechanism only as heuristic hiding. Supply a precise leakage model, fixed-versus-random TVLA, attack-success experiments, and repeated/chosen-input analysis.

---

### C3. The lazy-NTT safety proofs are invalid or incomplete

**Anchor:** Theorems 1–2, lines 127–137; proof, lines 152–158; NEON narrowing operations, lines 165–189.

Theorem 1 treats every Montgomery-product output as bounded by 1665 independently of the multiplicand. Under signed Montgomery reduction, the output bound includes an `|a|/2^16` term; growing lazy coefficients must therefore enter the recurrence.

Theorem 2 overlooks the GS branch

`r[j] ← r[j] + r[j+ℓ]`,

which can add two unreduced values. The stated `q + 2×1665` argument does not bound that branch. Exact twiddle extrema, signed representations, casts, narrowing, inverse scaling, and backend-specific arithmetic are absent.

**Minimum remedy:** Provide executable per-layer interval proofs for every scalar, NEON, and CUDA branch under exact C/intrinsic semantics. Include multiplication-dependent Montgomery bounds, additions, casts, narrowing, and inverse normalization. Validate using exhaustive/bounded checks, sanitizers, and adversarial edge inputs before claiming a formal proof.

---

### C4. The proposed NTT checksum invariant is apparently false

**Anchor:** Section V-B, lines 245–251; Tables VI and XVIII.

The manuscript asserts that the ordinary coefficient sum is invariant under the NTT. For a butterfly

`(a,b) → (a+ζb, a−ζb)`,

the output sum is `2a`, not generally `a+b`. An ordinary unweighted sum is therefore not NTT-invariant.

Moreover, the stated `1−1/q` detection probability requires a specified random-fault distribution and assumes the checksum computation and comparison are protected.

**Minimum remedy:** Supply the exact pre/post-transform checksum equations and proof, or replace the mechanism with a valid weighted checksum, residue code, or redundant computation. Define fault timing, location, value distribution, protected components, response behavior, and empirical injection coverage.

---

### C5. The GPU security boundary excludes the wrong operations

**Anchor:** Section VI-D, lines 335–340; Section VIII-D/E, lines 445–451; Algorithm 2.

The manuscript calls GPU KeyGen and Encaps “public-key operations” and permits their execution on an acknowledged non-constant-time GPU. KeyGen is secret-bearing: it processes secret seeds, noise polynomials, and the long-term decapsulation key. Algorithm 2 explicitly places `s` and `e` in GPU state.

Unified memory, device registers, shared memory, caches, driver access, DMA, co-tenants, and remanence are neither modeled nor zeroized.

**Minimum remedy:** Treat GPU KeyGen as secret-bearing. Define GPU trust, co-tenancy, software, and physical-observer assumptions. Either demonstrate appropriate leakage security or restrict GPU processing to genuinely public Encaps work. Explicitly synchronize and clear device, managed, and host-staging state.

# Major Findings

### M1. FIPS 203 fidelity is not established

**Anchor:** Abstract, lines 14–35; Section III-A; verification, lines 323–346; lines 431–432.

Equality with an unspecified pq-crystals Kyber version is not proof of FIPS 203 fidelity. Missing evidence includes:

- final ML-KEM domain separation and `G/H/J` behavior;
- encapsulation-key modulus and encoding checks;
- decapsulation-key consistency/hash checks;
- malformed-input and length handling;
- exact implicit-rejection behavior;
- randomness and system-call failure handling.

The “100 NIST KAT” corpus is not identified as official FIPS/ACVP material, and ordinary valid-input KATs cannot cover “all code paths.”

**Minimum remedy:** Pin all source commits, map implementation steps to FIPS 203 algorithms, publish vector files and hashes, use official ACVP/FIPS vectors where applicable, and add malformed-key/ciphertext and implicit-rejection tests for every backend/configuration.

---

### M2. SP 800-90B claims are technically inappropriate

**Anchor:** Section V-C, lines 253–273; lines 437–438.

SP 800-90B RCT/APT tests apply to raw noise-source samples with thresholds derived from assessed min-entropy. Applying ad hoc byte tests to conditioned Linux `getrandom()` output does not establish SP 800-90B entropy-source health.

The RCT probability is inconsistent: a fixed 24-byte all-equal run has probability `256^-23 = 2^-184`; the printed expression evaluates to `2^-176`. Stream length and overlapping windows are ignored.

The CBD sum monitor detects only limited mean shifts and can miss balanced or correlated faults. Its failure/retry behavior could also alter distributions or leak information.

**Minimum remedy:** Stop describing this as SP 800-90B compliance. Define the entropy architecture, startup/continuous state, cutoff derivation, total false-alarm rate, partial-read/EINTR handling, and latched failure policy. Analyze whether CBD monitoring conditions or leaks secret noise.

---

### M3. Constant-time evidence is assertion-only

**Anchor:** Section V-D, lines 275–279; Tables VI and XVIII.

A 64-bit comparison and compiler barrier do not prove branchless, address-independent execution under all supported compilers and optimization modes. Secret-derived shuffle accesses directly conflict with the broad constant-time claim.

**Minimum remedy:** Define the constant-time scope and leakage model. Publish relevant code and generated assembly, test all configurations with timing/leakage tools, and remove secret-dependent address schedules or explicitly exclude them from the claim.

---

### M4. Zeroization coverage is incomplete

**Anchor:** Table V, lines 211–220; Section V-D; lines 598–603.

Counting 17 stack objects omits compiler-created copies, registers and SIMD lanes, Keccak state, heap/managed memory, GPU state, error paths, and device caches. Volatile zeroing requires post-optimization evidence. Stack wiping alone does not justify the “cold boot ✓” claim.

**Minimum remedy:** Provide an object-lifetime inventory and all-exit-path analysis, use a documented non-elidable primitive, inspect post-LTO binaries, and include SIMD, host staging, unified memory, device buffers, and GPU intermediate state.

---

### M5. Compression mathematics is insufficiently specified

**Anchor:** Section IV-D, lines 173–175; Table III.

Constants alone do not establish equivalence. The manuscript omits offsets, rounding rule, input normalization, masks, signedness, intermediate widths, and treatment of all residue representatives.

**Minimum remedy:** State complete formulas for each compression/decompression operation and supply exhaustive equivalence tests over all 3329 residues for each backend, including maximum intermediate bounds.

---

### M6. Threat model and security comparisons are not credible

**Anchor:** Table VI, lines 222–242; Table XVIII, lines 502–520; Table XIX.

There is no coherent attacker-capability or trust-boundary model. Binary checkmarks present unvalidated heuristics as achieved security properties, while the “security feature count” treats incomparable mechanisms as additive. Competitor versions, configurations, and evidence are absent.

**Minimum remedy:** Replace counts and blanket checkmarks with scoped, versioned comparisons and evidence levels. Separate “implemented,” “tested mitigation,” “demonstrated resistance,” and “not covered.”

---

### M7. Reproducibility and benchmark comparability are inadequate

**Anchor:** Section VII-A, lines 309–320; comparative tables; availability statement, lines 642–644.

Missing information includes clock/power locking, CPU affinity, thermal state, distributions or confidence intervals, managed-memory page faults, synchronization boundaries, and whether every comparison measures the same complete FIPS operation. Cross-platform wolfSSL/liboqs/AVX2 numbers are not controlled.

**Minimum remedy:** Archive source, build scripts, vectors, and raw results. Report timing distributions, clocks, power mode, affinity and thermal conditions, and precise start/stop boundaries. Compare complete operations on the same hardware and security configuration.

---

### M8. Literature coverage contains material omissions and miscitation

**Anchor:** Section II and references 9, 12, and 13.

Bos et al. 2018 does not substantiate modern masked-implementation claims. Primas et al. is characterized imprecisely. Reference 13 concerns FPGA NewHope, yet supports the GPU-cryptography discussion. Relevant GPU ML-KEM/Kyber, masking, shuffling, fault analysis, formal reduction bounds, FIPS-transition implementation, and GPU leakage/remanence literature is absent.

**Minimum remedy:** Conduct a current, categorized literature review and moderate the “first” claim unless supported by a systematic search.

# Minor Findings

1. The manuscript alternates between six and nine hardening features: lines 24–26, 84–86, 222–230, and 605–607.
2. “Zero measurable runtime cost” conflicts with Tables X and XIX and the reported shuffle/wipe overhead.
3. “All code paths” and “all outputs through all KAT vectors” overstate what valid-input KATs establish.
4. Line 432 contains corrupted text (“P6FIPS 203”), and the entropy expression around lines 433–435 is malformed.
5. “Identical outputs for any correct Montgomery implementation” should distinguish mathematical congruence from identical intermediate representatives.
6. The MLWE/core-SVP figures require current estimator assumptions and citations.
7. “Highest performance” and “most comprehensive hardening” are unsupported by controlled comparisons.

## Author Questions

1. Does CUDA KeyGen return a 1152-byte K-PKE secret key or a complete 2400-byte ML-KEM decapsulation key?
2. Where are `z`, `H(ek)`, and full decapsulation-key assembly included in GPU timing?
3. Which exact FIPS 203 revision and pq-crystals commit are implemented?
4. How are final FIPS encapsulation-key checks, decapsulation-key checks, and `J` behavior handled?
5. How can a xorshift32-generated schedule possess over 1000 bits of entropy?
6. What is the precise shuffle domain and index-generation algorithm at each NTT layer?
7. Please provide exact generated per-layer coefficient bounds for scalar, NEON, and CUDA code.
8. What checksum values are compared, and how is invariance through a twiddled butterfly derived?
9. What happens after an RCT, APT, or CBD-monitor failure?
10. Which GPU and unified-memory objects contain secrets, and how are they cleared?
11. Are all headline timings complete FIPS operations with identical security settings?
12. Can code, vectors, raw benchmarks, disassembly, fault tests, and leakage traces be archived publicly?

## Recommendation

**Reject in present form, with encouragement to rebuild and resubmit.**

A substantially narrower performance paper may be viable, but publication with the current security and FIPS-conformance claims would require corrected mathematics, complete-operation benchmarks, a defensible threat model, leakage/fault evidence, and reproducible source and test artifacts.
